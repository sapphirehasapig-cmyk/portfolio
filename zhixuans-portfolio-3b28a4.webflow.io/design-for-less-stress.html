01
Why this, why now

Students are stressed. Nothing they use helps.

Everyone knows university is stressful. What surprised me: almost no one has a real tool for it.

60%
feel stressed every day.
Source: transformingeducation.org/student-stress-statistics
3.6%
use an app to manage it (my survey, 55 students).
My questionnaire, n = 55
Decision

Design for emotional support and peer connection — not another tracking or productivity tool.

Based on
Almost everyone is stressed, but only 3.6% use an app. Interviews showed students want to feel understood, not tracked.
Result
Dropped tracking and productivity features before designing any screens.
02
Who I designed for

Sarah, a first-year student figuring it out alone

Sarah is an international student juggling classes, a part-time job, and a new country. When stress piles up, she procrastinates — then feels guilty. When overwhelmed, she withdraws, right when talking to someone would help most.

She's not made up — she's built from patterns in 8 interviews and 55 survey responses with real DTU students.

Someone who gets it
Help managing time
A break from deciding alone
03
How I got there

Talking to students first, designing second

Before designing anything, I wanted to know what student stress really looks like.

8 one-on-one interviews
55 survey responses
Only 3.6% use a stress-relief app, even though almost everyone feels stressed. The need is there. The habit isn't.
Key finding from the questionnaire (n = 55)

All research followed GDPR: participants were briefed, data was anonymized, and deleted after the project.

04
The decision point

Two directions, tested head-to-head

Interviews and the survey turned up about 7 possible solutions. A workshop of 6 students scored them on a matrix, brainstormed and combined the strongest ones, then voted on 2 finalists — I prototyped both and had 9 more students test them.

6-person co-design workshop
9-person concept test
Chosen direction

Peer Support Group Chat

Join a small group for your specific stress — academics, money, relationships.

  • Scored highest on feeling creative and easy to understand
  • 5 of 9 students picked it as their favorite
Set aside

Calendar + Smart Reminders

One calendar, early reminders, a motivational nudge after each task.

  • Scored slightly higher on "fits my real life"
  • 4 of 9 preferred it — but most already had Google Calendar
Decision

Build peer support first. Shelve calendar integration.

Based on
Peer support scored higher on every rating — 4.67 vs 3.94 for how easy it was to understand, 4.39 vs 3.89 for how creative it felt — and 5 of 9 students picked it as their favorite.
Result
Peer support moved into prototyping. Calendar sync was dropped.

"We have a good network — we use our friends." — two participants, on why they preferred the calendar idea. The same words, from someone without that network, became the strongest case for peer support.

05
What I built

A place to find people who get it

The final app does one thing well, instead of trying to fix every kind of stress.

Decision

Keep the app to one job done well, not five jobs done half-way.

Based on
In the workshop, students rated AI matching as more useful than gamification or calendar syncing — those felt like nice extras, not something they actually needed.
Result
3 features: group support, AI matching, an anonymous wall.
01

Join a group for your exact stressor — like "Thesis Crisis Club" — not a generic forum.

02

Not sure which group fits? An AI assistant suggests one in seconds.

03

Anonymous Wall: no name, no photo — just a place to share and hear "me too."

Decision

Once the prototype was done, I went back and cleaned up the one-off screens into a small, reusable system.

Based on
One screen alone used 34 different colors, and nothing was set up to be reused.
Result
A shared set of colors, text sizes, and reusable pieces — buttons, cards, and other repeated bits I no longer had to redesign every time.

Scoped to one flagship screen as a proof of concept — the same token structure extends to the rest of the file.

06
Putting it in front of real students

One button, four failed attempts

7 students tried 4 tasks: find a group, ask AI for help, post anonymously, leave a comment.

7-person usability test

Finding a group and leaving a comment went fine. The other two didn't. 4 of 7 testers tried 3+ times and still couldn't find the AI help button — it was below the fold. Some also asked why "post anonymously" was buried in a menu called "Explore."

71%
already said the app felt genuinely helpful, in that same test, before anything was fixed (5 of 7).
2 / 2 / 3
on using it long-term: 2 yes, 2 no, 3 maybe. They wanted stress tracking and real counseling links.
Decision

Move AI Help from a buried menu item to a floating, always-visible button.

Based on
4 of 7 testers failed the AI-help task 3+ times — it was below the fold.
Result
AI Help became a floating button that's always visible — and the same pass simplified navigation, the post button, and post cards.
AI Help button
Now a floating button, always on screen, with a scroll indicator.
Navigation
Simplified to a top tab, renamed to a plain "Anonymous Wall."
Post button
Got a pencil icon, moved somewhere visible.
Post cards
Added "Likes" and "Comments" labels — no more guessing icons.
07
Who it landed with

"We have a good network — we use our friends. But it's a really nice idea for people who just moved here or don't have a social support network."

— a usability test participant

This came up again and again. The app resonated most with people who didn't already have someone nearby — new and international students, anyone starting over. It wasn't just about posting: several testers said just reading others' posts made them feel less alone.

08
What I'd do next

Still early — here's what's next

Test with a bigger, more diverse group — this round was 6–9 people, all from DTU.
Add a simple stress check-in. Testers wanted to track how they're doing, not just talk.
Connect groups to real university support services.
Design moderation for the Anonymous Wall — an open space needs safeguards.
